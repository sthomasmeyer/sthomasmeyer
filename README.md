### The Horsehead and Flame Nebulas

![The Horsehead and Flame Nebulas](https://assets.science.nasa.gov/dynamicimage/assets/science/cds/apod/apod/2026/november/Horsehead_Nebula_Killion.jpg?w=6000&h=4800&fit=clip)

Today's image could have been a painting. But what do these colors mean? The brightest star, left of the center, is blue Alnitak (the Girdle): a triple star system best known for being the easternmost star in Orion's Belt. The instantly recognizable shape of a dark horse's head at the center is the aptly named Horsehead Nebula. It is about five light-years tall and lies approximately 1,500 light-years away. It is made of dust and gas that obscure the red glow behind it, which is caused by hydrogen gas being ionized by the bright star Sigma Orionis at the top. The orange nebula with dark filaments on the left is the Flame Nebula. Closer to the Horsehead, the blue reflection nebula NGC 2023 is actively forming stars. The data for this "painting" were collected for almost six hours at the Utah Desert Remote Observatories, but the equipment was remotely controlled by the astrophotographer from his home, over 2,000 km away in Illinois.

Graham, The Atlantic, 17 Apr. 2026

_First things first: do not confuse upbraid with topknot lest you be upbraided for it. Topknot is a noun referring to a hairstyle, while upbraid is a verb (and an ancient one at that) meaning “to criticize or scold severely.” However, it may soothe your pride to know that the braid in upbraid likely comes from the same source as the verb braid, meaning “to do up (the hair) by interweaving three or more strands.” That source is the Old English word bregdan, which could be used to mean “to snatch,” “to move suddenly,” or “to plait,” i.e. “braid.” The Old English verb ūpbregdan is probably a combination of this bregdan with ūp, meaning “up.” If the connection between moving suddenly upward at someone and berating them seems obscure, you might consider upbraid to be a more formal counterpart of the expression “to get/be in someone’s face.”_
